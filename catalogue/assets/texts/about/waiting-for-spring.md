> “It was only in the 1940s that I began to explore twelve-tone music… My “Preludio e fuga” was composed between 1944 and 1947… But after the communist takeover in 1948, for fear of sanctions, I stopped using this technique… It was incidental music that, in 1954, allowed me to “flirt” with it again” [András Farkas: Mon père m’a raconté](https://www.editions-delatour.com/fr/biographies-entretiens/3727-mon-pere-m-a-raconte-la-vie-du-compositeur-hongrois-ferenc-farkas-9782752103079.html)
>
> Ferenc Farkas composed his cantata entitled "Tavaszvárás" (Waiting for Spring) in 1967, based on poems by Gyula Juhász. The titles of the four movements of the cantata perfectly reflect the atmosphere that Farkas wanted to give to his work:
>
> 1. “Invokáció” (Invocation) is imbued with a hymn-like and majestic character.
>
> 2. “Lirikus intermezzo” (Lyric Intermezzo): the poem by Juhász, which inspired this movement, is entitled “October”: the soothed melancholy, the golden light, the rural landscapes and the nostalgic beauty of autumn in this text are carried by the solo baritone.

{{% /column %}}
{{% column %}}

> 3. “Quasi scherzo”: the music, full of tenderness, refers to the poem from which the cantata takes its name.
>
> 4. “Epilógus” (Epilogue): Here, Farkas sets to music a poem entitled “To Auguste Forel,” which Juhász wrote in the aftermath of the First World War. In it, he pays tribute to the Swiss psychiatrist and scientist Auguste Forel, commending his lucidity and his commitment to pacifism in the face of the madness of the conflict. The text, which contrasts the destructive intoxication of war with the hope for a peaceful renewal, takes on the character of a celebratory chorale: “May life on earth be a festival, may justice be an ecstasy, and may man be worthy of man—free and happy. Amen.”
> {{< author name="Mihály Meixner" >}}
